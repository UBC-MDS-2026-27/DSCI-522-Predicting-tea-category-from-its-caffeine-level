Introduction (Clarissa)

Tea is a major source of caffeine and is highly consumed in China. Caffeine intake of up to 400 mg per day does not raise health concerns for healthy adults (Yong et al., 2022), but people who are sensitive to caffeine, or who need to limit it, have to judge how much they are taking in. Tea drinkers often use a tea’s category as a rough guide, assuming for example that white and green teas are low in caffeine and black teas are high. However, caffeine content varies with tea variety, geographical origin and season (Zhang et al., 2022), and a nationwide study of Chinese teas found that average caffeine content differed only modestly between categories, from about 27 mg/g for oolong to 43 mg/g for yellow tea (Yong et al., 2022).

In this project, we use a machine learning algorithm to predict a Chinese tea’s category (for example green, oolong or black tea) given its caffeine level. Answering this question is important because it tests how reliable category is as a guide to caffeine. If caffeine level strongly predicts category, the two carry overlapping information and category is a reasonable shortcut. If it does not, the common rule of thumb is unreliable, and people limiting their caffeine should look for caffeine information directly rather than relying on tea type.

To answer this question, we use The Chinese Tea Dataset. It contains 136 named Chinese teas, such as Xi Hu Longjing and Menghai 7542, each described by 11 columns: an identifier, English and Chinese names, category, growing region, caffeine level, body, oxidation level, main flavour notes, a short written description and a tier. Caffeine level is recorded as one of three values: low, moderate or high. The target we want to predict is the tea’s category, which has eight classes: green, white, yellow, oolong, black, dark, pu’er and scented tea.
